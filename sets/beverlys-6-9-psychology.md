# Beverly's 6–9 — Set Psychology

Three frameworks for understanding what this set can do to a room, and why the specific tracks matter.

---

## Context

This is a chop and screw set that does something unusual: it uses the aesthetic not just as a genre delivery mechanism but as a *transformation process* applied across wildly different source material — minimalist classical (Steve Reich), Soviet rock (Kino/Viktor Tsoi), Russian pop (Steklovata), post-grunge (Staind, Jesu), late-90s R&B (R Kelly), Southern rap (Project Pat, Gucci Mane), and abstract electronic (Autechre). The screwing/chopping is the constant. Everything else is in flux.

That's not a weakness. That's the argument.

---

## Model 1: The Neurochemical Arc
*Based on David Huron's ITPRA theory of musical expectation, and crowd psychology research on dopamine/cortisol cycling.*

The core mechanism: a crowd's emotional state is regulated by cycles of **anticipation → tension → release**. Dopamine governs reward and expectation. Cortisol governs arousal and stress. When you alternate between them deliberately, you hold a crowd in a state of engaged suspension — not comfortable enough to disengage, not stressed enough to leave.

The two poles the user identified — *intensity/discomfort* and *comfort/dissolution* — map directly onto cortisol spikes and serotonin/dopamine resolution. The mistake most sets make is staying in one register too long. The neurochemical model says: cycle, but at different scales simultaneously (micro = track-to-track, macro = set arc).

### This Set Through That Lens

**Opener: Steve Reich "Songs for Mallets" (screwed)**
Dopamine priming through repetition. Reich's minimalism is already built on delay and expectation — screwed, it becomes almost narcotic. The brain locks onto the pattern and starts generating prediction. You're loading the gun before anyone fires it. Correct opener.

**Dolby / Bowie "I'm Deranged" / Art of Noise "Moments in Love"**
Bowie's track is from *Lost Highway* — it was designed by David Lynch to produce dread through beauty. Art of Noise's "Moments in Love" is 11 minutes of slow dissolution at normal speed; screwed it becomes almost geological. These three together sustain the dopamine loop while introducing a mild cortisol undercurrent. The crowd is comfortable but slightly unsettled. That's exactly where you want them before you escalate.

**Eminem "Superman" / 10cc "Not in Love"**
Both songs are about emotional coldness and detachment — lyrically, structurally, sonically. "Not in Love" is particularly effective screwed: the original has background voices whispering "I'm not in love" in a way that already sounds like a hallucination. Slowed down, it becomes genuinely dissociative. This is still in the low-cortisol zone but the emotional temperature has dropped. The crowd feels this without naming it.

**Autechre "BAP Song" (delay FX)**
Cortisol begins to climb. Autechre is already destabilizing. With delay on top, the track loses its edges. This functions as a transition — a passage between the melodic comfort zone and what comes next. The FX choice here is important and correct: delay adds depth without adding aggression.

**Tupac "Run tha Streetz" / Project Pat "Gold Shine" (chopped and verb) / Gucci Mane "Dirty Lil Nigga"**
First real cortisol spike. Southern rap in chop and screw is the genre's home territory — the crowd (or at least part of it) recognizes the idiom. Tupac's posthumous register adds melancholy to the intensity. Project Pat in chopped and verb form is practically aquatic — hard content at half-gravity. Gucci is the peak of this cluster: most aggressive, shortest fuse. This is the first "squirm" moment.

**R Kelly cluster: Exit / Whole Lotta Kisses / Pregnant**
Serotonin restoration. R Kelly's late-90s production is lush, maximalist, and physically suggestive in ways that are distinct from threat. Screwed, these songs become slow and heavy in a specifically sensual register. Three in a row is a deliberate exhale. The crowd's cortisol drops. They settle back into their bodies. This also functions as social permission — the music is intimate, so the room becomes intimate.

**DMX "Slippin'" (dark)**
The most important track in the set from a neurochemical standpoint. "Slippin'" is DMX at his most confessional — addiction, failure, vulnerability, prayer. This is not intensity through aggression; it's intensity through exposure. The cortisol spike here comes from *recognition*, not threat. For a metropolitan crowd that has absorbed trauma as ambient experience, this lands differently than a hard rap track. It's the crack in the armor. It should be loud.

**Future "Purple Reign" (middle / mixed) / Future "Throw Away" / Future "My Collection"**
Future's auto-tune melancholy is uniquely suited to screwed aesthetics — he already sounds like he's moving through something thick. These three spread across the set function as a recurring tonal anchor. Purple Reign in particular with delay (1/8 30 32) in the middle placement is a structural hinge. The crowd is being told: we are moving through something.

**Steklovata "Dobraya Maya" & "Vesher" / Kino "Spokoinoy Nochi"**
Novel dopamine spike through cultural dislocation (see Model 2). Neurochemically: novelty triggers dopamine regardless of emotional content. The crowd cannot predict these tracks. That unpredictability is stimulating in a way that doesn't require aggression.

**Jesu "Sleeping In" / Staind "It's Been a While"**
Jesu is post-metal shoegaze — Justin Broadrick makes music that is already physically heavy and slow. Screwed it becomes glacial, almost subterranean. This is not a comfort track — it's a weight. Staind's "It's Been a While" as closer is correct: it's emotionally familiar to a wide demographic (post-grunge nostalgia), lyrically about absence and return, and at screwed speed becomes genuinely elegiac. The crowd ends in a state of recognized sadness rather than unresolved tension. That's a clean close.

**Recommendation for this model:** Consider moving DMX "Slippin'" later — perhaps after the R Kelly cluster rather than before it. The exposure moment lands harder when the crowd has been softened by intimacy first.

## Model 2: The Uncanny and Liminality
*Based on Freud's concept of the unheimlich (the uncanny), and Victor Turner's anthropological framework of liminality.*

**The Unheimlich:** Freud described the uncanny as the experience of something simultaneously familiar and strange — the home (*heim*) made unhomely (*unheim*). Chop and screw is a machine for producing this effect. You recognize the song. You cannot inhabit it normally. Your body's muscle memory for the track fires, but the tempo won't meet it.

**Liminality:** Turner described liminal states as "betwixt and between" — threshold moments when normal social structures are suspended. The club at 6–9pm on a Sunday is already liminal: not a worknight, not a night-night, ambiguously post-weekend. The screwed aesthetic compounds this. The crowd is between registers — between knowing the song and not knowing it, between movement and stillness.

### This Set Through That Lens

**Steve Reich "Songs for Mallets" (screwed)**
Reich's minimalism already occupies a liminal space between Western art music and African drumming — he was directly influenced by Ghanaian master drummers. Screwed, it becomes neither classical nor club music. It is genuinely threshold. This is an excellent choice for establishing the uncanny register before the crowd has expectations locked in.

**David Bowie "I'm Deranged"**
Already unheimlich at normal speed — it was composed for *Lost Highway*, Lynch's film about identity dissolution and doubles. The lyric "come with me / I'm fading" at screwed speed becomes something close to genuinely threatening. The crowd knows Bowie. They do not know this Bowie.

**10cc "Not in Love"**
One of the most uncanny originals in the set. The background voices in the original — women whispering "I'm not in love" — already read as dissociative at normal speed. Robert Smith recorded his own version, emphasizing the gothic dimension. Screwed: the voices become something the crowd can barely hold. This is the track where someone who doesn't know it will experience pure uncanny effect. Someone who does know it will experience the doubled uncanny of familiarity destroyed.

**Autechre "BAP Song"**
Autechre's music is already designed to frustrate pattern recognition. Screwed and delayed, it sits in a category the brain cannot locate. This is useful as a transitional track — it suspends categorization entirely, making the subsequent tracks land on a blank perceptual slate.

**Kino "Spokoinoy Nochi" (Good Night) — Viktor Tsoi**
The most extreme liminality in the set. Viktor Tsoi is a Soviet rock legend who died at 28 in 1990 — his face is still chalked on walls across former Soviet cities. The song has punk energy, Slavic melancholy, and the specific kind of political longing that has no equivalent in American music. Screwed, with the language barrier intact, the crowd receives pure affect with zero semantic anchor. They feel something they cannot name, in a voice that belongs to a dead man from another civilization. This is the most liminal moment in the set. Use it carefully — it can either open a room or lose it.

**Steklovata "Dobraya Maya" & "Vesher"**
Steklovata are a Russian pop group with a slightly campy, maximally sincere quality that reads as naive against American irony. Screwed, the naivety becomes gravitas. The contrast with Kino is important: Kino is serious art rock; Steklovata is Soviet pop. Both in the same set, both screwed, both in Russian — this creates a hall of mirrors around the concept of sincerity. The crowd cannot locate the irony because there isn't any.

**Metallica / Springsteen "Cindy" / Staind**
These three represent the uncanny through genre betrayal. Metallica in a screw set is not ironic — screwed, metal's aggression melts into something mournful. Springsteen represents blue-collar American vernacular emotion that typically does not appear in this context. Staind, whose emotional register is already stretched and confessional, screwed becomes something genuinely difficult to categorize. The crowd that hears these and recognizes them will experience something specific: the song they know has been taken somewhere they didn't know it could go.

**Recommendation for this model:** The Kino placement matters enormously. It should not be placed near other Russian tracks (don't cluster the "foreign music" together — that makes it a gimmick). Separate Kino from Steklovata by at least 6–8 tracks. Let each one land in a different emotional context.

## Model 3: Liquid Modernity and the Metropolitan Crowd
*Based on Zygmunt Bauman's theory of liquid modernity, Ray Oldenburg's concept of "third spaces," and Theodor Adorno's sociology of music listening.*

**Liquid modernity** (Bauman) describes the contemporary condition in which stable social categories — class, identity, cultural affiliation, temporal continuity — have dissolved into fluid, constantly renegotiated states. The metropolitan subject navigates a world of fragmentary belonging and ambient anxiety. Art that reflects this back without explaining it creates what Bauman calls "recognition without resolution" — a feeling of being seen that does not demand agreement.

**Third spaces** (Oldenburg) are environments that are neither home nor work — bars, coffee shops, clubs — where the ordinary social script is suspended and new forms of association become temporarily possible. Beverly's on a Sunday evening is a third space. The set is the architecture of that space.

**Adorno's distracted listening** — Adorno distinguished between concentrated listening (following the structure of a composition) and distracted listening (the crowd absorbing music as ambient affect). Chop and screw, particularly with this range of sources, makes distracted listening *the correct mode*. The set is not asking anyone to follow it. It is asking them to be moved by it without fully understanding why.

### This Set Through That Lens

**The Genre Mix as Social Statement**
Placing Project Pat next to Steve Reich, and R Kelly next to Kino, refuses the segregation of musical taste that mirrors social segregation. In liquid modernity, taste is one of the last arenas where people still perform fixed identity. This set destabilizes that performance. The crowd cannot arrive as "a rap person" or "an electronic person" and remain coherent. They have to dissolve a little.

**Slowing Time**
Liquid modernity is characterized by acceleration — the speeding up of capital, communication, and social change. Screw music is a direct refusal of that acceleration. It slows time. At Beverly's on a Sunday, already a day that resists productivity, this resonates as something close to protest. The crowd is not being asked to consume music efficiently. They are being asked to let it accumulate.

**Future (Throw Away / My Collection / Purple Reign)**
Future's position in contemporary metropolitan culture is specific: he is simultaneously a luxury symbol and a confessional artist about failure, addiction, and lovelessness. His auto-tune processing is already a form of liquid identity — the voice is real and processed simultaneously. Screwed, this ambiguity deepens. He is a useful figure for a Bauman-inflected set because he embodies the contradiction without resolving it.

**DMX "Slippin'" in Metropolitan Context**
DMX's confessional vulnerability — addiction cycles, religious longing, street violence as context rather than glorification — reads differently in 2024 than it did in 1998, and differently in a post-DMX death context. The metropolitan crowd at Beverly's has absorbed loss. This song names it without aestheticizing it. In Bauman's terms, it is a moment of "moral proximity" — the crowd is confronted with something real inside a space that usually holds them at aesthetic distance.

**The R Kelly Problem**
R Kelly is in the set. This is a deliberate choice that a sociologically-aware DJ should be conscious of. In a liquid modernity framework, the choice to play R Kelly (versus refusing to) is itself a statement about how the set treats the relationship between art and biography, between affect and accountability. The tracks chosen (Exit, Whole Lotta Kisses, Pregnant) are maximally sensual rather than maximally transgressive — this reads as deliberate curation rather than provocation. Worth knowing what you're doing here and why.

**Recommendation for this model:** Consider the specific composition of the room. Beverly's on a Sunday draws a particular crowd — not purely hip-hop, not purely electronic, not racially or class-uniform. The genre mixing is most effective as social architecture when the crowd is heterogeneous. If the room is homogeneous, the disorientation effect weakens and some of the more alienating choices (Kino, Jesu, Autechre) may simply lose the room rather than expand it.

## Cross-Model Observations

**Bizzy Bone "When Thugz Cry"** doesn't appear in the current set order but is in the master list. Bone Thugs' melodic rap — harmonized, mournful, already operating somewhere between R&B and rap — screwed is one of the most emotionally direct options available. It would work well immediately after the R Kelly cluster or as a bridge into the Russian material.

**The two Project Pat versions (chopped/unchopped)** as consecutive or near-consecutive tracks is an interesting structural choice. It functions as a demonstration — "here is the transformation." For an audience that doesn't know the screw aesthetic, this is educational. For one that does, it's a formal gesture. Worth thinking about whether demonstration serves the emotional arc or interrupts it.

**Jesu "Sleeping In"** is the most genre-defiant track in the set after the Russian material. Post-metal shoegaze has a specific physical effect — it is *heavy* in a way that creates somatic response (the body responds to sub-bass and distortion regardless of cognitive categorization). Screwed, this becomes almost gravitational. It should not be used as background — it requires the room's attention, and it will either get it or clear it.

**The overall shape** of this set as currently ordered moves from dissolution (opening ambient/minimalist material) through a series of intensification clusters with comfort returns, toward an ending that is elegiac rather than triumphant. That is the correct shape for a Beverly's Sunday set. You are not sending people into a night — you are closing something.

## Addendum: The Temporal Dimension

*On the question of what extended song length does to the analysis.*

Chop and screw typically reduces playback to 60–70% of original speed. A 3-minute song becomes roughly 4.5 minutes. A 6-minute song becomes 9. "Moments in Love" by Art of Noise, already 11 minutes at normal speed, becomes 16–17 minutes screwed. This is not a minor variable. It is the frame around everything else.

### The Three-Phase Residency Model

Standard music listening at normal tempo rarely forces a crowd past what could be called **phase two** of emotional engagement:

- **Phase 1 — Recognition:** the crowd identifies what the track is doing to them emotionally. "This is sad." "This is tense."
- **Phase 2 — Processing:** the crowd holds the emotional state consciously, comparing it to memory, context, expectation.
- **Phase 3 — Surrender:** the processing stops. The crowd stops *having* the feeling and simply *is* in it. Self-consciousness fades. The room becomes the music.

Most songs at normal tempo plateau in Phase 2 before the track ends — there isn't enough time to push past active processing into surrender. Screwed tracks systematically extend the time spent in any given state, which means Phase 3 becomes achievable within a single song. This is the actual psychological mechanism behind why screw music feels different in the body, not just different in the ear. You are not listening to it. Eventually, you are inside it.

This changes every model.

### How It Changes Model 1 (Neurochemical Arc)

The cortisol/dopamine cycling described previously assumed transitions happen at a pace similar to normal listening. Extended duration changes the shape of the cycle:

The body **habituates** to sustained cortisol exposure after roughly 3–4 minutes of the same intensity. A screwed track that runs 6–8 minutes in an intense register will push past the initial stress response into a second state — closer to acceptance or dissociation than to fight-or-flight. This is physiologically different from a brief cortisol spike. The crowd isn't anxious; they're submerged.

Conversely, extended **comfort tracks** allow actual physiological recovery — not just a brief respite before the next intensity cluster, but a genuine reset of the autonomic nervous system. Three R Kelly tracks each running 5–6 minutes means the comfort phase is 15–18 minutes long. That's enough time for the room's average cortisol level to genuinely drop, which makes the subsequent emotional disruption (DMX "Slippin'") land from a cleaner baseline — more exposed, less defended.

**The specific risk:** Art of Noise "Moments in Love" at 16+ minutes as track five. This is a significant durational ask before the crowd has committed to the set. It will either complete the early-set trance state or lose the room to distraction. There is no middle outcome at that length.

**The specific opportunity:** DMX "Slippin'" extended to 6+ minutes means the confession doesn't end before it finishes working. The crowd cannot wait it out. They have to go through it. The difference between a 4-minute exposure and a 6-minute exposure to that level of emotional nakedness is not linear — it's the difference between observing something and becoming implicated in it.

### How It Changes Model 2 (The Uncanny)

Freud's *unheimlich* is typically a moment — a flash of disorientation. Extended screwed duration transforms the uncanny from a moment into a **sustained condition.** The crowd cannot recover their footing between the recognition of strangeness and the end of the track. They must live in the uncanny for longer than is comfortable.

This is most consequential for the foreign-language tracks. Kino's "Spokoinoy Nochi" at normal speed is a 3-minute Soviet punk song with a forward momentum that gives the crowd a handrail — even without understanding the words, the pace and structure are legible. Screwed to 4.5–5 minutes, that handrail disappears. The track becomes genuinely strange for longer than the crowd can politely hold the strangeness at arm's length. They will either fully enter it or fully reject it — and extended duration removes the third option of waiting it out.

This applies equally to Jesu. At normal speed, heavy shoegaze is unusual but navigable. Screwed, at potentially 8–10 minutes, it becomes a durational ordeal — in the specific sense of a trial that changes you if you complete it. The crowd that makes it through Jesu screwed is a different crowd than the one that went in. Something has been established about what this room is willing to receive.

**Practical implication:** the placement of both Kino and Jesu must account for room fatigue. These are high-demand tracks that require the crowd to have reserves. Neither belongs after a long, emotionally draining cluster. They need to land when the crowd has energy to spend on strangeness.

### How It Changes Model 3 (Liquid Modernity)

Bauman's core argument about liquid modernity is that the contemporary subject has been trained toward **short-cycle attention** — brief engagements, rapid transitions, constant novelty as the baseline condition. The smartphone has accelerated this to an almost physiological level. The average person in 2024 has difficulty sustaining focused attention on a single thing for more than a few minutes without the compulsion to switch.

Extended screwed tracks are, from this perspective, a form of **enforced presence** — not aggressive, not demanding, but simply refusing to end when the crowd's conditioned attention expects relief. This is the set's deepest countercultural act. Not the genre mixing, not the slowing, but the duration. The crowd that stays in a screwed Art of Noise track for 16 minutes has done something their daily life has systematically trained them not to do: they have **waited without switching.**

This creates a specific social phenomenon in the room. People who would normally manage discomfort by reaching for their phones find themselves not doing it — not because they've decided not to, but because the room's collective commitment holds them. Extended duration is socially binding in a way that brief tracks are not. The crowd makes a small collective agreement each time a track extends past the point of expected resolution, and those accumulated small agreements build the social fabric of a Beverly's Sunday set.

**The sunk cost of emotional investment:** this is not manipulative, but it is real. Once a crowd has spent 4 minutes inhabiting DMX's "Slippin'", they are less likely to emotionally exit at minute 5 than they would have been at minute 1. Duration creates commitment. The longer the crowd has been in a state, the more the transition out of that state means — which means the arrival of the next track lands harder, whatever it is.

### The Central Revision

The original analysis treated the set as a sequence of emotional states. With duration factored in, it is more accurate to describe it as a sequence of **residencies** — extended inhabitations of specific emotional conditions. The crowd is not moving through the set. They are being asked, one track at a time, to move in and stay a while. Some rooms will accept this. Some won't. The rooms that do will end the night having experienced something that a normal-tempo set structurally cannot offer: the specific altered state that only comes from having been somewhere, emotionally, for long enough that you can't pretend you were just passing through.
